Makeshift signs are information architecture showing up after the launch. Kate Canales's work on handwritten public instructions makes the repair visible: when doors, bathrooms, payment terminals, or institutional processes do not explain themselves clearly enough, people add the missing labels, cautions, arrows, and context.

That does not mean every handmade sign is proof of a failed designer. It means the real use environment contains more edge cases, local norms, and moment-by-moment confusion than the formal system anticipated. The note taped to the machine is a situated taxonomy: it names the problem in the user's language, orders the next action, and lowers the cost of recovery for the next person.

Canales's TED framing is useful because it treats these annotations as humane design evidence, not visual noise: "[[cite:ted_canales_makeshift_signs_quote]]" For IA and UX practitioners, the lesson is to study the unofficial instruction layer before replacing it. Those scraps often contain the clearest map of where the system's model and the user's context diverge.
